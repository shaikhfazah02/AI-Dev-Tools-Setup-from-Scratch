# LinkedIn Organic Content Strategy for B2B SaaS — Playbook & SOP

**Topic:** LinkedIn organic content strategy for B2B SaaS
**Research base:** 10 experts, 30+ sources (YouTube transcripts, podcast appearances, blog articles, LinkedIn posts)
**Repository:** All source material is in /research/

---

## How to Use This Playbook

This is not a list of tips. It is a set of decisions — each one grounded in what practitioners who have actually built audiences and pipeline through LinkedIn recommend, where they agree, and where they don't. Every recommendation cites its source so you can verify the reasoning yourself.

---

## Core Recommendations

### 1. Do the positioning work before writing a single post

Before deciding what to post, get clear on three things: who exactly you are talking to, what you believe about the market that others don't, and what your buyer would do if your product didn't exist. Skipping this step is the single most common reason content campaigns produce activity but no pipeline.

> Source: Michelle J. Raymond — "The 2026 LinkedIn Playbook: What B2B Marketers Need to Do Differently" (youtube.com/@MichelleJRaymond) — "Stop creating content. Do the positioning work first."
> Source: April Dunford — "Obviously Awesome: April Dunford's 2026 Positioning Do-Over" (youtube.com/@positioningshow) — documents four prerequisite decisions before any positioning exercise begins.

---

### 2. Invest in personal profiles, not the company page

Personal profiles consistently outperform company pages by 3–10x in engagement, and company page organic reach now sits under 2%. The company page has one job: legitimacy. Keep it professional and current. All content investment should go into the founder's and team members' personal profiles.

> Source: Michelle J. Raymond — CBRE case study article (linkedin.com/pulse, June 10, 2026) — a 3,500-follower personal profile got 300% more clicks than the same post from a 2-million-follower company page.
> Source: Richard van der Blom — "The 2026 Playbook for LinkedIn Authority and B2B Growth" (GrowEurope, youtube.com) — company page organic reach under 2% in 2026.

---

### 3. Write the hook first — or don't write the post at all

The opening line is not the introduction to the post. It IS the post, from a distribution standpoint. LinkedIn's algorithm rewards dwell time — how long someone stays reading before scrolling. A mediocre idea with a great hook will outperform a great idea with a weak one. Do not write a post until you have the hook.

> Source: Adam Robinson — "How I Grew RB2B to 30 Million Dollar ARR Using LinkedIn" (youtube.com/@AdamRobinson) — "We won't write a post if we don't have the hook first."
> Source: Justin Welsh — "My Writing Process: 162 Million Impressions" (research/other/) — entire writing framework (Meat, Trailer, Summary) built around hook lines that each earn the next line of attention.

---

### 4. Track impressions vs. engagement — not likes alone

Likes measure public engagement. They do not measure reach or influence. For B2B SaaS specifically, the people most likely to become buyers — HR leaders, COOs, founders evaluating tools — are the least likely to publicly like or comment on content about their pain points, because doing so signals vulnerability. A post with 15 likes and 40,000 impressions is not underperforming. It may be your best post.

> Source: Sam McKenna — "Building Your Personal Brand On LinkedIn" webinar (youtube.com/@hashtagsamsales) — dark social formula: likes divided by impressions below 0.01 means content is traveling off-platform, shared in Slacks and internal threads.

---

### 5. Post consistently for months before judging results

Nearly every expert in this research warned against measuring LinkedIn performance on a 30-day horizon. The real audience — the people who will eventually become customers — reads everything and engages with nothing publicly, sometimes for months, before reaching out. The silent community is the real pipeline.

> Source: Richard van der Blom — "The 2026 Playbook for LinkedIn Authority and B2B Growth" — a global marketing director followed his content for six months before emailing about a specific project, having never liked or commented once.
> Source: Adam Robinson — linkedin.com/posts/retentionadam (autonomous business post, 2026) — describes posting for a full year before finding content-market fit.

---

### 6. Activate employees with enablement, not advocacy software

Tools that push pre-written company posts for employees to reshare produce low engagement and damage authenticity. What works: give employees the context, prompts, and frameworks to develop their own genuine point of view, then help those voices reach further. Executives get full drafts written for them. Subject-matter experts get prompts, not scripts. General employees get 3–5 copy options with brackets to personalize.

> Source: Michelle J. Raymond — "The 2026 LinkedIn Playbook" (youtube.com/@MichelleJRaymond) — tiered employee activation system (executive drafts, SME prompts, employee copy options).
> Source: Chris Walker — "LinkedIn Organic Isn't Dead" (youtube.com/@chriswalker171) — explicitly calls out canned advocacy tools as ineffective; employees need real thoughts to share, not marketing team captions.

---

### 7. Give away 95% of your knowledge for free

Clients in 2026 do not pay for information — they pay for help implementing it. If your content teaches everything you know, it does not eliminate the need to hire you. It increases it, because readers now understand the complexity of what they're trying to do. Gate as little as possible.

> Source: Richard van der Blom — "The 2026 Playbook for LinkedIn Authority and B2B Growth" (GrowEurope, youtube.com) — "Give away 95% of knowledge for free. Clients pay for implementation, not information."

---

### 8. Use AI to structure your ideas — not to generate them

AI-written LinkedIn content is now detectable, and detection damages credibility. The right use of AI in content creation is to structure raw ideas you have already formed — voice notes, rough thoughts, messy drafts — into polished posts, with a human editing and approving the final version. The AI should be writing as you, not instead of you.

> Source: Simon Hoiberg — "Yap-to-content" LinkedIn post (linkedin.com/in/simonhoiberg, 2026) — full AI content workflow: voice memo, Telegram, OpenClaw agent trained on his brand brief, FeedHive draft in his exact writing style.
> Source: Chris Walker — linkedin.com/posts/chriswalker171 (AI slop post, 2026) — "60%+ of LinkedIn comments are now automated AI slop... people should get banned for this."

---

### 9. Build a "silent community" activation habit

The people most likely to become customers never publicly engage — they read everything quietly, sometimes for months, then reach out directly. Build a weekly habit: check who viewed the founder's profile in the last 90 days, filter for ICP matches (HR leads, COOs, founders at scaling companies), and send a light no-pitch message — "noticed you stopped by, happy to help if useful." The content does the warming; this move does the conversion.

> Source: Richard van der Blom — "The 2026 Playbook for LinkedIn Authority and B2B Growth" (GrowEurope, youtube.com) — a global marketing director followed his content for six months before emailing about a project, never having engaged publicly once.
> Source: Sam McKenna — "Building Your Personal Brand On LinkedIn" webinar (youtube.com/@hashtagsamsales) — describes CMOs at Fortune 500s who read everything, engage with nothing, and show up in profile views months before buying.

---

### 10. Tell the story of why you built it — not what it does

Product-focused content consistently underperforms story-driven content. People don't share feature lists — they share perspectives, beliefs, and origin stories. The "why behind the product" is almost always more compelling on LinkedIn than the product itself. Lead with what you believe about the problem, not what your solution does.

> Source: Dave Gerhardt — "Brand is all that matters right now" (linkedin.com/in/davegerhardt, 2026) — "the last great differentiator just might be brand... the founder is actually a good person and kind and funny and authentic."
> Source: Lenny Rachitsky — Tony Fadell interview (youtube.com/@LennysPodcast) — "Storytelling is an essential skill for builders, because humans are wired for narrative, not feature lists. The key is telling the why, not just the what."

---

### 11. Sequence your channels intentionally — don't copy-paste across all of them

Running a company page, personal profiles, and paid in parallel with identical content is the most common LinkedIn mistake in B2B. Each channel has a specific job: the company page establishes credibility and anchors the brand, paid extends reach to a precisely targeted audience, and personal profiles carry the trust and perspective that a logo can't. Sequence them — don't duplicate them.

> Source: Michelle J. Raymond — CBRE Power of Two article (linkedin.com/pulse, June 10, 2026) — "Before your next campaign, ask one question of every piece of content: which job is this doing, and which channel does that job best?"
> Source: Chris Walker — "LinkedIn Organic Isn't Dead" (youtube.com/@chriswalker171) — runs both company page and personal profile in parallel but with distinct roles, not identical content.

---

### 12. Have a clear market point of view — not just a product POV

In a noisy, AI-disrupted market, a clearly stated belief about where the industry is going is more valuable than any product claim. What do you believe about the future of your market that others don't? That belief — stated confidently and repeatedly — is what builds an audience of people who think the same way, and those are exactly the people most likely to buy.

> Source: April Dunford — "When we have big changes in a market, like now with AI, it's important to communicate a clear point of view about the future of the market" (linkedin.com/in/aprildunford, 2026).
> Source: Dave Gerhardt — "There is something crazy about just being so in your face with the content" (linkedin.com/in/davegerhardt, 2026) — argues that authentic market perspective beats fear-based or product-first messaging every time.

## Where Experts Disagree

### Disagreement 1: Video vs. Text — Which format actually works on LinkedIn?

**What Adam Robinson and Chris Walker recommend:**
Both argue that video is a primary trust-building format. Robinson says video creates a layer of connection that text alone can't match — voice is deeper than text, and video is the whole thing. Walker credits video as a major driver of Refine Labs' growth and treats it as a long-term brand asset.

> Source: Adam Robinson — "How I Grew RB2B to 30 Million Dollar ARR Using LinkedIn" (youtube.com/@AdamRobinson)
> Source: Chris Walker — "LinkedIn Organic Isn't Dead" (youtube.com/@chriswalker171)

**What Sam McKenna recommends:**
McKenna argues the opposite for most creators: 90%+ of LinkedIn video is watched on mute, it forces people to consume at the video's pace rather than skim, and most executives without media training come across as scripted rather than authentic. A stop-the-scroll text hook plus an image or infographic will outperform video for the great majority of content creators on this platform.

> Source: Sam McKenna — LinkedIn post (linkedin.com/in/samsalesli, 2026) — "video CAN work well for specific use cases... but a stop-the-scroll text hook + an image or infographic will work over video, for the great majority of content creators on this platform."

**My take:**
McKenna is right for most B2B SaaS companies at an early content stage. Video pays off once a founder's voice is already recognized — it accelerates trust that already exists. Before that, it's a high-effort format with unpredictable returns. Start with text and a strong hook. Add video once the audience is there.

---

### Disagreement 2: AI in content creation — use it or avoid it entirely?

**What Adam Robinson recommends:**
Robinson avoids AI for writing entirely. His argument: the human perspective — imperfect memory, real experience, genuine opinion — is the actual product. AI can't replicate the point of view of the artist. Audiences can feel the difference, and AI-written content consistently underperforms.

> Source: Adam Robinson — "What actually IS an autonomous business?" (linkedin.com/in/retentionadam, 2026) — describes building RB2B's content entirely on authentic human storytelling.

**What Simon Hoiberg recommends:**
Hoiberg's entire content operation is AI-driven — voice memos during mountain walks go into Telegram, an OpenClaw agent structures the thoughts, and FeedHive (pre-trained on his brand brief and writing style) produces a full week of content. He argues this is still authentic because the AI is writing as him, not instead of him.

> Source: Simon Hoiberg — "Yap-to-content" LinkedIn post (linkedin.com/in/simonhoiberg, 2026) — "Still my ideas. But the AI did the heavy lifting."

**My take:**
Both are right — but they're describing different things. Robinson is warning against using AI to generate opinions from scratch. Hoiberg is using AI to structure ideas he already formed. The distinction matters: AI as a drafting and structuring tool (applied to your own raw thoughts) is legitimate. AI as an idea generator is what damages credibility. The process Hoiberg describes — voice memo first, AI structures second, human reviews third — is a reasonable workflow for a small team trying to post consistently.

---

### Disagreement 3: How much should you invest in growing your company page?

**What Michelle J. Raymond and Chris Walker recommend:**
Both make a case for running the company page seriously. Raymond frames it as the "anchor" — it builds legitimacy, feeds the employee advocacy program, and gives personal brands something credible to reference. Walker treats it as algorithm insurance and runs it in parallel with his personal profile.

> Source: Michelle J. Raymond — "The 2026 LinkedIn Playbook" (youtube.com/@MichelleJRaymond) — "I view the Company Page as the anchor. It's sort of that foundation that makes everything else credible."
> Source: Chris Walker — "LinkedIn Organic Isn't Dead" (youtube.com/@chriswalker171)

**What Sam McKenna and Richard van der Blom recommend:**
McKenna is blunt: personal content will always outperform company content, and the ROI of investing heavily in the company page is low. Van der Blom's data is the hardest number in this research: company page organic reach now sits under 2% in 2026.

> Source: Sam McKenna — "Building Your Personal Brand On LinkedIn" webinar (youtube.com/@hashtagsamsales)
> Source: Richard van der Blom — "The 2026 Playbook for LinkedIn Authority and B2B Growth" (GrowEurope, youtube.com) — company page organic reach under 2% in 2026.

**My take:**
Keep the company page professional and current — it's where people verify you're real, and a neglected page damages credibility. But don't expect it to drive reach or pipeline. The time investment should go into personal profiles. Raymond and Walker are right that it serves a legitimacy function; McKenna and van der Blom are right that it shouldn't be the primary content vehicle. These aren't contradictory — they just assign the company page a narrower, more honest role.

## What I Rejected and Why

### Rejected Idea 1: Comment baiting ("Comment X to get the slides")

Several creators in the B2B LinkedIn space use comment-baiting as a reach tactic — asking followers to comment a specific word or phrase in exchange for a resource (e.g., "Comment PLAYBOOK and I'll send you the guide"). The logic is that comments boost algorithmic reach, so engineering comments artificially inflates distribution.

**Why I rejected it:**
Richard van der Blom's 2026 Algorithm Insights Report — the most data-driven source in this research, built on 2.5M+ posts — explicitly flags this as a tactic LinkedIn now actively penalizes. Accounts using comment baiting risk having their reach restricted. Beyond the platform risk, it attracts low-quality engagement from people who want the freebie, not from genuine ICP members. The silent community finding makes this even clearer: the people most likely to become customers don't comment anyway. Engineering comments from the wrong audience while risking account restrictions is a bad trade.

> Source: Richard van der Blom — "The 2026 Playbook for LinkedIn Authority and B2B Growth" (GrowEurope, youtube.com) — comment baiting is actively penalized by LinkedIn in 2026 and can result in account restrictions.

---

### Rejected Idea 2: Employee advocacy software

Multiple vendors sell "employee advocacy" platforms that distribute pre-written company content to employees for easy resharing — one click, done, reach multiplied. On paper, it scales the company's LinkedIn presence without requiring employees to create anything original.

**Why I rejected it:**
Chris Walker explicitly calls this out as ineffective, and Michelle J. Raymond's tiered activation system implicitly rejects it entirely. The problem is structural: pre-written posts reshared by employees produce near-zero engagement because LinkedIn's algorithm (and the audience) can detect inauthenticity. Fifty people posting the identical caption on the same day signals coordination, not genuine conviction. Worse, it burns employee goodwill — people resent being used as distribution channels for marketing content they didn't write and don't believe in. What actually works is enablement: giving employees frameworks and prompts to develop their own point of view, then supporting those genuine voices. That's harder to scale, but it's what produces real engagement.

> Source: Chris Walker — "LinkedIn Organic Isn't Dead" (youtube.com/@chriswalker171) — employees need real thoughts to share, not marketing team captions to repost.
> Source: Michelle J. Raymond — "The 2026 LinkedIn Playbook" (youtube.com/@MichelleJRaymond) — tiered enablement system replaces advocacy tools entirely.

## My Original Ideas

### Original Idea: The "Hiring Signal" Content Calendar

None of the 10 experts in this research discussed timing LinkedIn content around external buying signals — they all focused on consistency, cadence, and format, but treated the content calendar as a fixed, evergreen schedule regardless of what's happening in the market.

My idea: for a B2B SaaS company in the hiring space like 100Hires, the content calendar should be dynamically tied to publicly available hiring signals — not just posted consistently, but posted strategically when the target audience is most actively feeling the pain the product solves.

**How it would work:**

1. **Monitor funding announcements** — companies that just raised a Series A or B are almost always about to hire aggressively. A post about hiring at scale, published the week a batch of funding announcements drops, lands in front of exactly the right people at exactly the right moment.

2. **Track LinkedIn's own "We're hiring" signals** — when companies in your ICP start posting job ads at volume, that's a real-time signal that they're in hiring mode. Content about hiring pain (sourcing, speed, quality of candidates) published in that window is more likely to be shared internally by the exact people experiencing it.

3. **Seasonal hiring peaks** — Q1 (January–March) and Q3 (July–September) are historically the two biggest hiring windows across most industries. Content campaigns planned around these windows — rather than distributed evenly across the year — would concentrate effort when buyer intent is highest.

**Why it could work:**
Every expert in this research agrees that the silent audience is the real pipeline — people read, don't engage, and reach out when they're ready. This idea doesn't change that dynamic; it just increases the probability that the right content lands in front of the right person at the moment they're most ready to act. It turns a passive content strategy into one that's loosely synchronized with real buying intent — without requiring any paid targeting or sales outreach.

**Why it's not in the research:**
The experts in this research are personal brand builders and LinkedIn strategists — they teach content systems that work for any topic. None of them are building for a product category (hiring tech) where external market signals are this legible and this directly tied to buyer intent. This idea is specific to 100Hires' context, not a general LinkedIn tactic.

## Weaknesses of This Playbook

### 1. Most of the research is skewed toward personal brand builders, not B2B SaaS companies

The majority of experts in this research — Justin Welsh, Adam Robinson, Sam McKenna, Richard van der Blom — built their LinkedIn presence as individual operators or consultants, not as employees of a B2B SaaS company with a product to sell, a team to manage, and investors to answer to. Their advice is genuinely valuable, but it was developed in a context where the founder IS the brand, the product is often the person's knowledge, and there's no sales team, no marketing budget approval process, and no competing internal priorities. Applying their frameworks to a funded SaaS company with a 10-person team requires more translation than this playbook explicitly provides.

### 2. The "silent community" finding is compelling but hard to measure

Multiple experts independently described the same pattern: most future customers never publicly engage, read quietly for months, then reach out directly. This is almost certainly true — but it's also conveniently unfalsifiable. It's easy to look at low engagement numbers and tell yourself "the right people are watching silently." That may be true. It may also mean the content isn't resonating with anyone. This playbook doesn't resolve that tension, and any team using it should build in an honest checkpoint at 90 days: are silent readers actually converting, or is "they're watching quietly" just a comfortable story about underperforming content?

### 3. The algorithm findings have a short shelf life

Richard van der Blom's Algorithm Insights Report is the most data-driven source in this research — but it's a snapshot of how LinkedIn's algorithm behaved in 2025–2026. LinkedIn has changed its algorithm significantly multiple times in the past five years. Any specific tactical advice in this playbook that references algorithm behavior — saves outperforming comments, personal profiles vs. company pages, carousel performance — should be re-verified against current data before being treated as settled fact.

### 4. No data from 100Hires' own account

Every recommendation in this playbook is based on what worked for other people in other contexts. None of it has been tested against 100Hires' actual audience, existing content, or current LinkedIn presence. The honest starting point is to treat this as a set of informed hypotheses, not proven tactics — and to build a lightweight testing process (post 2–3 variations of the same idea, compare impressions vs. engagement ratios, double down on what moves) rather than committing fully to any single approach before it's been validated in-context.

### 5. LinkedIn posts were collected manually, not via API

LinkedIn posts for all 10 experts were manually copied from their profiles rather than collected via API, because LinkedIn does not offer a public content API and automated scraping violates their Terms of Service. This means the post sample (3 posts per expert, 30 total) is a small, manually selected snapshot — not a statistically representative picture of each expert's full content strategy. A more rigorous research base would analyze 50–100 posts per expert over 6–12 months to identify genuine patterns rather than relying on a handful of recent examples.

## Who I Would NOT Recommend Following for This Topic — and Why

### Primary: Lenny Rachitsky (Lenny's Podcast)

Lenny Rachitsky is one of the most respected voices in B2B SaaS product and growth — his newsletter has 1.2 million subscribers, his podcast guests are consistently world-class, and his thinking on product strategy, retention, and growth frameworks is genuinely excellent.

He is not the right person to follow for LinkedIn organic content strategy.

His LinkedIn posts — including the three collected in this research — are summaries of podcast conversations with other people (Tony Fadell, Benedict Evans). They are well-written and widely shared, but they work because Lenny already has 150,000+ followers and an established brand. The format (long structured takeaway lists from interviews) is almost impossible to replicate for someone building from zero, and his content gives no tactical guidance on LinkedIn specifically — how the algorithm works, what formats drive reach, how to write a hook, how to activate employees, or how to convert silent readers into pipeline.

Following Lenny for LinkedIn content strategy is like following a Michelin-star chef to learn how to meal prep on a weekday. The quality is real, but the context is so different it provides almost no actionable guidance for someone at an earlier stage.

**Who to follow instead:** Richard van der Blom for algorithm-specific data, Justin Welsh for a replicable personal brand content system, Michelle J. Raymond for B2B Company Page and employee advocacy strategy.

---

### Secondary flag: Chris Walker (with caveats)

Chris Walker is a genuinely strong voice on B2B demand generation and LinkedIn strategy — his data (engagement rate up 104% even as post views dropped 78%) is compelling, and his critique of vanity metrics is one of the sharpest observations in this research.

The caveat: his LinkedIn content has increasingly shifted toward personal development, lifestyle philosophy, and mindset content (his "10 habits that block your potential" post being a clear example). This content performs well for him because his audience already knows and trusts him as a B2B marketing operator. For a B2B SaaS company trying to build credibility in the hiring space, replicating his current posting style — mixing marketing commentary with personal growth content — risks confusing the audience about what the brand actually stands for.

Walker is worth following for his strategic thinking on demand gen and LinkedIn's role in the B2B buying process. He is not worth emulating as a content style reference for a company in an early brand-building stage.
